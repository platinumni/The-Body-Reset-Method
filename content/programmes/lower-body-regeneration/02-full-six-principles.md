Every exercise in this programme is built on the same six principles, in the same order. Understanding them is what turns a set of exercises into a method.

## 1. Recognise

Recognising works both ways.

First, you become aware of the tension, gripping, compensation and habitual patterns you've built up: toes clawing at the floor, knees locked, hips tightened, the breath held.

Second, and just as important, you learn to recognise the muscles that genuinely should be working. Often these are the very muscles your compensation has been covering for. You can't use the right muscles until you know which ones they are.

## 2. Release

Release happens in layers.

**First, release the compensation.** Let go of the muscles that have been doing a job that isn't theirs.

**Then, release the excess, even in the muscles you need.** Most of us use far more effort than a movement actually requires. The goal is to find the equilibrium point: where your body is supported and stable, but not forced.

> Keep what the movement needs. Let go of everything else.

### An example: standing on one leg

Standing on one leg, you need:

- steady pressure through the big toe and the little toes
- your stabilising muscles switched on
- your hips level and square

That's all the balance needs. But most people also brace through the back, let the spine drift out of line, tense the shoulders and hold their breath. None of that helps the balance. It's extra effort the body has learned to add. Release means keeping the first list and letting go of the second.

## 3. Reconnect

With the excess effort gone, you can start to feel again. You reconnect with each specific part of a movement: where the pressure sits in your foot, which muscles switch on and when, and how one part of the body hands the movement on to the next.

You don't just do the movement. You understand it from the inside.

## 4. Retrain, 5. Strengthen

The first exercises in each stage focus on recognising, releasing and reconnecting. Only once those foundations are in place does the work move on to retraining your movement patterns and progressively building strength and balance.

Because that strength is built on muscles that are no longer fighting each other, it supports you instead of reinforcing old habits.

## 6. Integrate

At the advanced levels, the exercises deliberately add complexity. You might keep a steady breathing pattern while you count a rhythm as you raise and lower your heels, holding your awareness on several things at once.

At first, your mind will find this genuinely hard. That's normal: there's a lot for your nervous system to organise at the same time. This is known as **dual-task training**. It teaches your body to keep simple movements well controlled while your attention is busy elsewhere, which is exactly what everyday life asks of it.

## Putting it together

As you work through the stages, keep asking yourself three questions:

1. What am I gripping or forcing that I don't need to?
2. Which muscles should be doing this work?
3. Can I do the same movement with less effort, and still stay stable?
