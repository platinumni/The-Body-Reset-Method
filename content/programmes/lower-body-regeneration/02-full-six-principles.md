Every exercise in this programme rests on the same six principles, taken in the same order. Understanding them is what turns a set of exercises into a method.

## 1. Recognise

Recognising works in two directions.

First, you become aware of the tension, gripping, compensation and habits you've built up over the years: toes clawing at the floor, knees locked, hips tightened, the breath quietly held.

Second, and just as important, you learn to recognise the muscles that genuinely should be working. Often these are the very muscles your compensation has been covering for. You can't use the right muscles until you know which ones they are.

## 2. Release

Release happens in layers.

**First, release the compensation.** Let go of the muscles that have been doing a job that isn't theirs.

**Then, release the excess, even in the muscles you need.** Most of us use far more effort than a movement requires. The goal is the equilibrium point: where your body is supported and stable, but not forced.

> Keep what the movement needs. Let go of everything else.

### An example: standing on one leg

Stand on one leg and notice what happens. The ankle, knee and hip line up, and the stacking continues all the way up: the pelvis, the ribs, the spine and the head, each balanced softly on the one below.

At first, your toes will grip the floor to hold you up, especially the big toe. That's natural, and it's useful. It's the consciously competent stage: you're gripping, and you've got it.

Then you begin to release even that grip. Not completely, and not all at once. You want the grip available the moment you need it, with the flexibility and adaptability to engage it only when it's necessary. As you sway to the left or right, you'll feel it engage too much. Let it, then release again. Gradually, your balance reactions and coordination take over, and your body settles, almost by itself, into its equilibrium point.

Meanwhile, most people are also bracing through the back, tensing the shoulders and holding the breath. None of that helps the balance. It's effort the body has learned to add. Release means keeping what the balance needs and letting go of everything else.

## 3. Reconnect

With the excess effort gone, you can begin to feel again. You reconnect with each part of a movement: where the pressure sits in your foot, which muscles switch on and when, and how one part of the body hands the movement on to the next.

You don't just do the movement. You understand it from the inside.

## 4. Retrain

Retraining is where you take that new awareness and practise simple movements with better coordination. You're replacing old patterns with new ones, slowly and deliberately, until the new way begins to feel like your own.

The movements here are deliberately simple. Simplicity leaves room for attention, and attention is what rewires a habit. Done slowly and well, a few repetitions teach your nervous system more than many done on autopilot.

## 5. Strengthen

Only once the foundations are in place does the work turn to building strength and balance, progressively and patiently.

Because that strength is built on muscles that are no longer fighting each other, it supports you instead of reinforcing the old habits. And because it's built in the position you have rather than the range you wish you had, it's strength you can trust.

## 6. Integrate

Integration is where it all comes together.

At the advanced levels, the exercises deliberately layer complexity onto simple movements: a steady breath, a count, a hold, your awareness spread across several parts of the body at once. At first your mind will find this genuinely hard, because there's a lot for your nervous system to organise. This is often called **dual-task training**. It teaches your body to keep simple movements well controlled while your attention is busy elsewhere.

But integration reaches far beyond the exercises. It's the same concept everywhere: in the way you stand and walk, the way you climb the stairs, the way you lift your children or your grandchildren, the way you carry the shopping. It's in your golf swing, your martial arts, your running, your game, whatever you love to do. Release what isn't needed, engage what is, and trust your body to do the rest.

## Putting it together

As you work through the stages, keep asking yourself three questions:

1. What am I gripping or forcing that I don't need to?
2. Which muscles should be doing this work?
3. Can I do the same movement with less effort, and still stay stable?
